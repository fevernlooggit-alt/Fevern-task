# 未完之线 · The Unfinished Line

**A design philosophy for works that are deliberately half-born.**

The Unfinished Line is a movement devoted to the moment a drawing is still becoming. It refuses the tyranny of the completed image. Its artifacts stop on purpose — mid-feather, mid-fold, mid-thought — because the most generous thing a drawing can do is leave room for another hand. Every sheet in this movement is a duet score: one voice already played, one voice left silent and waiting. The work must feel meticulously crafted precisely so that its silences read as invitation, never as neglect.

Space is organized like a draughtsman's worktable: a single monumental figure holds the center, while satellites of study — details, structures, alternates — orbit it in quiet marginal columns. Composition follows the logic of the technical plate: framed vignettes, dimension ticks, numbered guides, registration marks. Nothing overlaps; everything breathes. The page reads as an instrument of observation, an artifact from an imaginary discipline that studies grace with calipers. This clinical scaffolding is what makes the romance of the subject bearable — and believable.

Color is nearly abstinent. Warm paper, graphite grays, and a cool "non-photo" blue-gray for construction lines — the color of thinking. One drop of dark cinnabar red is permitted per sheet, placed like a seal: small, ceremonial, final. Material honesty is absolute: every stroke must look like it came from a pencil held by a person — doubled passes, faint wobble, pressure that tapers. The grain of the paper is part of the drawing. Master-level execution here means the wobble is calibrated, not accidental; each tremor placed with painstaking attention.

Rhythm comes from repetition with drift: rows of feathers, links of chain, ranks of hatching — systematic accumulations of small marks that reward long looking, each one minutely different, the way a patient expert's hand is never a machine. Scale contrasts are steep: one large complete passage of finished linework beside vast passages of pale scaffold. The eye must always be able to tell three states apart at a glance — *finished*, *under construction*, and *blank* — because the entire meaning of the sheet lives in that gradient.

Typography whispers. Small labels, thin weights, generous letter-spacing; numerals set like instrument readings. Text exists only to name parts and give quiet instruction — never to explain. Words sit in the margins as a surveyor's notes sit beside a mountain: respectful, brief, exact.

The final artifact must look labored over for countless hours by someone at the absolute top of their craft — and then, at the height of their power, they set the pencil down and slid the sheet across the table to you. The masterpiece is the handover. Finish it.
